# Unit 0.1 FAQ and Unstuck Guide

This guide addresses frequent stumbling blocks encountered when analyzing OmniSupply Operations and authoring the Client Brief.

## The Zero-Jargon Trap <a id="zero-jargon-trap"></a>

### Symptom: Using AI buzzwords instead of describing operational workflow bottlenecks

Submissions that use words like "LLM", "AI agent", "machine learning", or "vector database" fail the rubric immediately.

### Why this fails the rubric

A client brief defines the business problem, the document workflow, and the operational constraints. It is not an implementation proposal. When you write "we deploy an autonomous agent to read invoices", you conceal the work the software must perform:

1. You hide the intake complexity. An operations manager needs to know how scanned PDF files, carbon-copy packing slips, and mobile photos are processed.
2. You make premature technical commitments. Stating that an LLM will solve reconciliation assumes probabilistic text generation is appropriate before validating whether deterministic database queries or optical extraction rules are required.
3. You prevent non-technical leaders from reviewing your document. The compliance officer and operations director cannot verify whether an abstract model architecture satisfies their governance policies.

### How to fix it

Replace computational mechanisms with business domain operations:

- Instead of "we use an LLM to parse invoices", write "the pipeline extracts line items, quantities, and unit prices from incoming vendor PDF invoices".
- Instead of "we use a RAG agent to detect discrepancies", write "the system compares extracted invoice line items against corresponding purchase orders and contracts to flag unit price variances".
- Instead of "an autonomous agent decides approvals", write "the workflow approves conforming claims under agreed dollar limits and routes disputed claims to an operations specialist".

Describe physical documents, data fields, validation checks, and human roles. If a sentence requires a software engineering background to understand, rewrite it in operational terms.

## Resolving Trilemma Conflicts <a id="trilemma-conflicts"></a>

### Symptom: Conflating the compliance officer's audit requirements with the operations manager's speed metrics

Students often attempt to satisfy the Operations team by proposing fast auto-approvals while ignoring the Compliance team's requirement for verified contract clauses.

### Why this breaks the business

Operations wants dispute turnaround cut from 72 hours to under 2 hours. Compliance demands that 100% of price adjustments and credit memos cite specific contract clauses.

- If you prioritize only Operations, you auto-approve merchant claims without contract verification. The company pays fraudulent or invalid claims that suppliers refuse to reimburse, causing direct financial leakage.
- If you prioritize only Compliance, you require manual specialist inspection of every line item and contract page. The backlog remains two to three days deep, merchants withhold payments on five-figure invoices, and partners churn.

### How to resolve the conflict

The solution is a two-track routing architecture governed by quantitative validation:

1. Conforming Path (Fast Track): When an incoming document matches the purchase order perfectly, complies with all vendor warranty timelines, and falls below established risk ceilings, the system generates the adjustment and logs the verified contract clause reference automatically. Operations achieves rapid resolution, Compliance receives a full audit trail with clause citations, and Finance maintains predictable unit costs.
2. Exception Path (Escalation): When a claim exhibits line-item price variances, missing delivery timestamps, or high dollar amounts, automated approval stops. The file routes immediately to a human specialist with pre-extracted evidence. Operations avoids manual data entry on standard cases, while Compliance and Finance retain human governance over high-risk decisions.

## Quantitative vs Vague HITL Boundaries <a id="hitl-boundaries"></a>

### Symptom: Drafting vague escalation rules without numeric boundaries

Submissions that propose rules like "escalate expensive claims" or "flag suspicious supplier behavior" fail rubric verification.

### Why vague rules fail

Software pipelines cannot branch on qualitative descriptions. An instruction to "escalate high-value disputes" cannot be implemented in a validation schema or routing policy. Furthermore, vague criteria leave operations specialists uncertain about their daily responsibilities and prevent automated verification from scoring the brief.

### What makes a threshold quantitative

A valid Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) escalation rule requires three distinct components:

1. An unambiguous numeric cutoff: A specific dollar value, percentage variance, time interval, or transaction count.
2. A business rationale: An explanation of the specific risk or failure mode that triggers the stop.
3. A designated human destination: The exact operational specialist, department, or queue responsible for resolving the exception.

### Comparison examples

| Vague Rule (Fails Rubric) | Quantitative Rule (Passes Rubric) |
|---|---|
| Escalate large credit requests to management. | Any merchant credit claim or invoice adjustment exceeding $500.00 must halt automated processing and route to the Senior Dispute Specialist. |
| Flag invoices with price differences. | Any unit price discrepancy between the invoice line item and the purchase order exceeding 5.0% or $50.00 routes to the Purchasing Specialist. |
| Review suppliers who dispute too frequently. | Any supplier whose dispute rate exceeds 8.0% of total invoice volume over a rolling 30-day window diverts all pending invoices to the Compliance Review Queue. |
| Check claims that arrive close to the deadline. | Any damaged freight claim submitted within 48 hours of the supplier contractual filing deadline routes to the Priority Claims Coordinator. |
