## Sentence-level mechanics

- **Open sections conversationally.** A short, almost-spoken opener ("So,
  let's be real.") works well for the pain section specifically — don't
  reuse it verbatim, find the instructor's own version of a direct opener.
- **Alternate short and long sentences.** A run of same-length sentences
  reads flat. Follow a longer explanatory sentence with a short, blunt one.
- **Use rhetorical questions sparingly**, addressed straight at the reader,
  to voice the exact thought they're having ("Sound familiar?" only works
  once per page, if at all).
- **First-person plural for the learning journey, first-person singular for
  the instructor's own claims.** "We'll build a mental model together" vs.
  "I've been writing CSS for fifteen years." Don't blur the two.
- **Direct address beats hedged address.** "You've been stuck on this"
  lands harder than "Many developers find themselves stuck on this."

## The litmus test

Before keeping any sentence, ask: **would this sentence still be true,
word-for-word, if you swapped in a totally different course on a different
subject?** If yes, it's generic filler — replace it with a detail specific
to _this_ course, instructor, or subject.

"This course will help you master the fundamentals and take your skills to
the next level" passes for literally any course ever made, which is exactly
why it convinces no one. "This course will help you stop copy-pasting
Stack Overflow answers you don't understand" only fits one kind of course,
which is why it works.

## The banned-phrase list

These aren't banned because the words are forbidden in isolation — they're
banned because they're almost always doing the _generic-filler_ job the
litmus test catches. If a specific, proof-backed sentence happens to contain
one of these words, that's fine; the failure mode is using them to paper
over the absence of a real detail.

**Transformation hype** — "unlock your potential," "unleash your inner
[X]," "supercharge your career," "transform the way you [X]," "take your
skills to the next level" (as vague filler; a _specific_, proof-backed claim
like "next-level layout animations" describing something actually shown is
fine).
→ Replace with the literal, specific thing the reader will be able to do.

**Empty intensity** — "game-changing," "revolutionary," "groundbreaking,"
"cutting-edge," "world-class" (unless immediately backed by a specific
proof point in the same sentence).
→ Replace with the concrete fact that would make a reader conclude
"world-class" on their own.

**Vague ease claims** — "the easiest way to learn X," "master X in just
[N] days," "no experience necessary" used to paper over real difficulty.
→ Either state the real time/effort honestly, or don't make a
speed/ease claim at all.

**Corporate throat-clearing** — "in today's fast-paced digital world," "now
more than ever," "look no further," "in this day and age."
→ Delete. Start the sentence at the actual point.

**Filler adjectives** — "seamless," "robust" (unless literally describing a
technical property), "comprehensive" (demonstrate it via curriculum detail
instead of claiming it), "powerful," "amazing," "incredible" used as
throwaway intensifiers.
→ Replace with the specific detail that earns the adjective, or cut the
adjective.

**False urgency** — "don't miss out," "limited time only," "act now"
(unless literally true and stated with a real number — a real cohort start
date, a real number of seats left).
→ State the real constraint, or drop the urgency framing entirely.

**Third-person brand voice** — "[Course Name] empowers students to...",
"Our proven system helps learners...".
→ Rewrite in first person. The instructor is a person talking to another
person, not a brand describing its own value proposition.

**Empty CTAs** — "Get Started Today!", "Enroll Now and Transform Your
Career!", "Don't Wait — Sign Up!"
→ A plain, short CTA label ("Enroll Now", "Register", "Join the Waitlist")
does more work than a hyped-up one. The hype belongs in the body copy,
earned with detail — not stapled onto the button.

## Before/after rewrites

Four original examples, deliberately spanning different subjects to show
the pattern generalizes beyond programming courses. None of this text is
from a real course — write fresh copy for the user's actual subject rather
than adapting these.

**1. Programming**

> Generic: "Master JavaScript with our comprehensive, expert-led course
> designed to unlock your potential as a developer."

> Reworked: "You've watched a dozen JavaScript tutorials, and you can
> still get tripped up by `this`. That's not a you problem — it's a
> teaching problem. I want to fix that."

**2. Visual art**

> Generic: "Our watercolor course will transform your painting skills with
> easy-to-follow, professional techniques."

> Reworked: "Watercolor punishes hesitation. One tight, careful brushstroke
> and the paper remembers it forever. Most beginners respond by painting
> scared — small, timid strokes — and wonder why their work looks stiff.
> There's a reason for that, and it's fixable."

**3. Public speaking**

> Generic: "Learn public speaking and become a confident, powerful
> communicator."

> Reworked: "You know your material cold. You've rehearsed it in the
> shower a dozen times. Then you stand in front of six coworkers and your
> voice does that thing. I used to have the exact same problem, and it
> wasn't about confidence — it was about breath."

**4. Data analytics**

> Generic: "This bootcamp offers cutting-edge, industry-relevant training
> for aspiring data analysts."

> Reworked: "Most 'Intro to SQL' tutorials teach you SELECT statements
> against a tidy, twelve-row sample table. Then you get a real job, open a
> production database with forty undocumented tables, and freeze. This
> course is built entirely around that second moment."

## Punctuation and formatting conventions

- Prefer periods and commas to em dashes. When a sentence wants a dramatic
  pause, end it and start a new short sentence instead — it reads as more
  natural, spoken cadence, and avoids the flattened effect of leaning on one
  punctuation mark throughout a page.
- Use real typographic quotation marks for pull-quotes.
- Use numbered or bulleted lists for scannable specifics (curriculum
  sub-topics, "who this is for" personas) — not for emotional or narrative
  passages, which should stay in prose.
- Use ALL CAPS or exclamation points sparingly; when used, they should read
  like genuine spoken emphasis, not marketing volume.

## Emoji and humor calibration

- Default: one or two emoji per major section at most, placed at a genuine
  emotional beat (relief, excitement, warmth) — never decorating a bullet
  list or heading.
- Self-deprecating humor about the instructor (a past mistake, an
  awkward photo, early naivety about the subject) is a strong trust-building
  device — but it has to be true and specific to _this_ instructor. Ask
  what's actually true and funny about them rather than inventing a
  persona or borrowing someone else's joke.
- If the subject matter is sensitive (health, grief, financial hardship,
  trauma-related topics), drop emoji and jokes almost entirely — see
  SKILL.md Section 0 on reading the emotional register the brief calls for.
