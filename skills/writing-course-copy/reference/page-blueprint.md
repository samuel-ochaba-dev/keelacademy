For each section: its purpose, the real input to gather from the user before
drafting it, and original heading-pattern examples. Not every course needs
every section — cut what doesn't apply rather than padding a short course
page out to match this list.

## 1. Header

**Purpose**: minimal, gets out of the way. Login link (if the course has a
platform) plus one primary CTA button.

**Input needed**: course name, CTA label. Keep the label to two or three
words ("Enroll Now", "Join the Waitlist", "Register Now").

## 2. Hero

**Purpose**: state the transformation in one bold, concrete headline, then
let a one-sentence subhead scope who it's for.

**Input needed**: the single sharpest problem statement the course solves;
the instructor's name and a one-line credibility tag.

**Heading patterns** (originals — write a fresh one, don't reuse these
verbatim):

- A command naming the pain directly: "Stop wrestling with [subject]."
- A warm reframe: "The joy of [subject]."
- A concrete promise with a built-in qualifier: "[Skill], the way [audience]
  actually needs to learn it."

**Byline pattern**: "Taught by [Name]" plus a one-line photo caption — warm
or self-deprecating if the instructor is comfortable with that register,
otherwise a plain credibility tag ("15 years building production [X]
systems").

## 3. Pain section

**Purpose**: this is the empathy engine for the whole page. Get it right and
everything after it lands harder.

**Input needed**: the specific failure mode this audience experiences. Ask
directly — "what does it look like when someone struggles with this? What
have they usually already tried?"

**Structure**: two to four short paragraphs.

1. Open with a direct, almost-spoken sentence (a "So, let's be real."-style
   opener is optional but effective — find the instructor's own phrasing).
2. Describe the frustration in sensory or behavioral terms, not
   abstractions — what does the reader _do_ when stuck (rewatch tutorials,
   copy-paste without understanding, avoid the task entirely)?
3. Name the emotion explicitly, then immediately reframe it as structural
   rather than personal.

## 4. Reframe + personal pivot

**Purpose**: turn from empathy to offer, in as few words as possible.

**Pattern**: one short paragraph restating that the subject is actually
learnable or enjoyable once the hidden structure is visible, followed by a
one-line paragraph carrying the pivot itself ("I want to help," or an
equivalently short, plain line in the instructor's own words — don't reuse
this exact phrasing, find their version of it).

## 5. Method differentiation

**Purpose**: prove, don't just claim, why this course teaches differently.

**Input needed**: an actual example of an exercise, interactive element,
project, or teaching device that's genuinely distinctive. If the course is
a standard video course with no distinguishing method, don't fabricate an
interactivity claim — differentiate on something else that's real (depth,
instructor access, project specificity, community quality).

## 6. Trusted-by / logo strip

**Purpose**: a fast, low-effort credibility signal.

**Input needed**: real company names or logos of where alumni work, or real
notable endorsers. If none exist yet, omit the section entirely — a
course's first cohort doesn't need a fabricated logo wall (see the
overriding rule in SKILL.md).

## 7. Curriculum

**Purpose**: walk the subject like a syllabus, building trust through real
structure rather than a bullet-point ad.

**Structure**: open with one or two sentences framing the overall arc or
ambition of the curriculum, then list modules in teaching order using the
per-module template in SKILL.md Section 4.

**Input needed**: the real module list and, for each, the real specific
sub-topics — not vague category names.

## 8. Capstone projects / deliverables

**Purpose**: make the outcome tangible. Not "you'll learn skills" but
"you'll have built these specific things."

**Input needed**: the real projects or deliverables. Name them like real
things ("a clone of [X]," "a working [Y]"), not vaguely ("a real-world
project").

## 9. Bonus content

**Purpose**: reward-stacking for the top pricing tier, when the course has
tiers.

**Input needed**: what the actual bonuses are, and which tier includes them.

## 10. Team / group pricing

**Purpose**: a B2B upsell block, when applicable.

**Input needed**: real discount tiers or percentages, minimum seat counts.

## 11. Testimonials (second placement)

**Purpose**: broaden social proof beyond famous names. Real, relatable
students — with enough personal detail (role, location, a specific
before/after) to feel specific rather than generic — build more trust here
than a second round of industry names.

**Input needed**: real testimonial text with attribution (name plus role,
or a note that it's anonymized on request). Never invent a quote, ever,
even a placeholder-styled one with a fake name — mark unfilled slots
clearly as `[TESTIMONIAL NEEDED]` instead.

## 12. Instructor bio

**Purpose**: humanize the person behind the course, using the arc in
SKILL.md Section 5.

**Heading pattern**: usually its own warm, informal heading ("Hi, I'm
[Name]!").

**Input needed**: real bio facts — career history, what pulled them into
the field, a specific project or moment that demonstrates expertise.

## 13. FAQ

**Purpose**: close every objection honestly, using the default question set
in SKILL.md Section 6.

**Input needed**: the real answers — pricing, refund terms, format details.
Don't guess at policy specifics (refund windows, regional pricing terms,
payment plans) — ask.

## 14. Final CTA + footer

**Purpose**: close the page the way it opened — plain and warm, no
last-minute high-pressure tactics ("last chance!!"). A short closing line
plus the CTA button again is enough.
