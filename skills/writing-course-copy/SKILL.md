# Writing Developer-Educator Style Course Copy

This skill teaches the landing-page voice used by well-known interactive-course
educators — most visibly Josh W. Comeau's _The Joy of React_, _CSS for
JavaScript Developers_, and _Whimsical Animations_ — reverse-engineered from
their public pages into a reusable technique. It's a **pattern and a voice**,
not a copy of anyone's actual sentences. Never lift phrasing, taglines,
testimonial text, or jokes from Comeau's or anyone else's real pages — write
fresh copy for the user's own course that follows the same underlying
mechanics.

This style fits any expertise-driven, skill-based course taught by a real,
credible practitioner: programming, design, writing, music, trades, fitness,
data, language learning. It doesn't fit corporate LMS copy, university
marketing, or hype-driven "guru" launch copy — Section 0 covers reading
whether it's the right fit for a given brief.

## The rule that overrides everything else

Never invent a testimonial, a company logo, a student count, a completion
time, or any other proof point. If the user hasn't supplied it, write
`[NEED: student count]` (or similar) inline and ask for the real number, or
omit the element entirely. This style works _because_ it reads as radically
honest — one invented quote or fake "trusted by" logo, if a reader ever
checks, undermines the whole approach and misleads real buyers. This has no
"just for a mockup" exception; draft mockups with clearly-labeled placeholder
content instead of invented specifics.

## 0. Read the brief before writing a word

Real specifics are what make this copy work — a generic brief produces
generic copy no matter how well the voice rules below get applied. Before
drafting, gather or confirm:

- **The subject and the learner's actual struggle.** Not "learn Python" —
  what specifically makes it hard for _this_ audience? What's the
  tutorial-hell pattern in this field?
- **The instructor's real credibility and real origin story.** Where did
  they actually work, build, or struggle? A genuine specific ("built X at Y
  for Z years") beats vague authority ("industry expert").
- **What makes the teaching method different, if anything** — interactivity,
  projects, community, sheer depth. If the course is standard video
  lectures, say that; don't claim interactivity that isn't there.
- **Who this course is explicitly _not_ for.** Every strong course page
  names its non-audience. Ask the user this directly rather than guessing.
- **Real numbers**: lesson/video count, hours, price, refund window,
  testimonials, company logos. Placeholder anything missing per the rule
  above.

State a one-line **voice read** before drafting, the way you'd confirm a
brief with a client:

> _Reading this as: an intermediate-level course on [subject] for
> [audience], teaching-voice register [warm / dry / energetic — pick one],
> leaning on the instructor's real credibility in [specific thing]._

If the subject calls for a different emotional register than Comeau's
upbeat energy — a course on grief-support facilitation, financial-hardship
recovery, trauma-informed movement — say so, and dial back the
enthusiasm, jokes, and emoji accordingly. The underlying mechanics (specific
pain → honest reframe → personal pivot → concrete method → transparent FAQ)
still apply; the surface energy shouldn't be forced onto a mismatched topic.

## 1. Why this voice converts

Generic course copy sells the _outcome_ with abstract superlatives ("master
JavaScript," "become a data science expert," "elevate your career"). This
style instead sells the _method_, using specific, checkable detail, and
trusts the reader to conclude the outcome follows. That's the whole
mechanism, and every section below is built around specificity over
adjectives as a result.

The emotional arc running through the page:

1. **Name the exact pain, precisely.** Not "learning is hard" — the
   specific failure mode of _this_ subject.
2. **Reframe the pain as structural, not personal.** The reader doesn't lack
   talent; the subject was taught badly, or has hidden systems nobody
   explained. This removes shame, which is what actually blocks most adult
   learners.
3. **Pivot to "I want to help" — briefly, plainly.** No fanfare. This is the
   turn from empathy to offer.
4. **Prove the method, don't just claim it.** Show, don't tell: describe an
   actual exercise, mini-game, or project — not "engaging content."
5. **Walk the curriculum like a syllabus, not a bullet-point ad.** Real
   module names, real topics, one sentence of "why this matters" each.
6. **Let credibility be specific and slightly self-deprecating**, never
   grandiose.
7. **Close every objection in the FAQ, honestly** — including admitting what
   the course doesn't cover.

## 2. Voice rules

The full word-level guide — banned-phrase list, before/after rewrites across
several non-programming subjects — lives in
[reference/voice-rules.md](reference/voice-rules.md). Read it before drafting
hero copy or curriculum blurbs. Condensed here:

- **Address**: "I" for the instructor's own claims and story. "We" for the
  shared learning journey ("we'll build...", "we'll discover..."). "You" for
  the reader's own experience. Avoid third-person brand-speak ("[Course]
  helps students...").
- **Sentence rhythm**: mix short declarative sentences with longer
  explanatory ones. A one-sentence paragraph is a valid emphasis tool — use
  it at the pivot moment, not everywhere.
- **Emphasis**: bold or italicize a word or short phrase _inside_ a
  sentence to carry weight — not the whole sentence, and not more than once
  or twice per paragraph.
- **Concrete beats abstract, always.** A real number, tool name, or module
  title outperforms an adjective. Catch yourself writing "powerful,"
  "seamless," or "cutting-edge" and replace it with the specific thing that
  makes it true.
- **Admit limitations on purpose.** State plainly what the course doesn't
  cover. This isn't modesty for its own sake — it's the biggest trust
  signal in this voice, and its absence is what makes generic sales copy
  feel untrustworthy.
- **Emoji**: at most one or two per major section, placed at a genuine
  emotional beat, never as decoration on a bullet list.
- **Humor**: optional, self-deprecating, aimed at the instructor — never at
  the reader's struggle.
- **Never condescend.** The reader's confusion gets validated, not mocked,
  even lightly.

## 3. Page architecture

The full annotated blueprint — exact heading patterns and what real input
each section needs from the user — lives in
[reference/page-blueprint.md](reference/page-blueprint.md). Skeleton, in
order:

1. Minimal header (course name / login, one CTA button)
2. Hero: bold value-prop headline + one-sentence subhead + instructor byline
3. Pain section: name the specific struggle
4. Reframe + personal pivot ("I want to help")
5. Method differentiation, proven with one concrete example
6. Trusted-by / social-proof strip (real logos or quotes only)
7. Curriculum, module by module (Section 4 below)
8. Capstone projects / deliverables, if the course has them
9. Bonus content, if any
10. Team / group pricing, if applicable
11. More testimonials — ideally broader, less-famous "real student" voices
12. Instructor bio (Section 5 below)
13. FAQ (Section 6 below)
14. Final CTA + footer

Not every course needs every section — a $49 mini-course doesn't need a
team-pricing block. Cut what doesn't apply rather than padding.

## 4. The curriculum module pattern

```
### Module N [Short, concrete module name]

[1-2 sentences, "we" voice, naming what's actually learned and why it's
tricky or important — not a topic label]

**[One bolded/italicized sentence raising the stakes or previewing the
"aha" — the module's emotional hook, in plain language]**

[Optional: 2-4 bullets of specific sub-topics, real terms not vague
categories]
```

Example (original, for a fictional course — never copy Comeau's actual
module text):

> **Module 3 — Joins Without Tears**
>
> Most SQL courses throw four kinds of joins at you at once and hope
> something sticks. We're going to build up from a single INNER JOIN, one
> mental picture at a time, until LEFT, RIGHT, and self-joins all feel like
> the same idea wearing different hats.
>
> _By the end of this module, you'll be able to look at a messy join and
> know exactly which rows are about to vanish, before you run the query._
>
> - Reading a join like a Venn diagram instead of memorizing syntax
> - The most common join bug (and why it never throws an error)
> - Self-joins, explained with an org chart instead of an abstract example

## 5. The instructor bio arc

Pattern: **origin story hook → brief self-deprecating aside (optional) →
credentials as a scannable bullet list → warm, direct closing line to the
reader.**

- The origin story is a specific memory ("In [year], my [employer] asked me
  to..."), not a resume summary.
- Credential bullets mix impressive facts with humanizing ones — a company
  name lands harder next to a specific project than in a plain logo list.
- The closing line addresses the reader directly and restates why the
  instructor cares, in one sentence specific to _this_ course — skip
  generic sign-offs like "Can't wait to see what you build!"

## 6. The FAQ pattern

The FAQ is where the honesty principle does its heaviest lifting. Default
question set — skip what doesn't apply, add subject-specific ones:

- Is this course up to date? (state plainly what isn't covered, if anything)
- Is this course right for me? (a "good fit" list _and_ a "might not be for
  you if..." list)
- How much prior experience do I need?
- How is this different from other courses on the subject?
- What's the refund policy? (real terms, no hedging)
- Is there a community, or can I ask questions if I get stuck?
- Pricing/format specifics (tiers, installments, team licenses, regional
  pricing)
- Certificate of completion, if applicable
- One question that admits a real limitation of the course

Answer every entry the way you'd answer a skeptical friend directly — plain,
specific, no spin. If the honest answer is "no, we don't do that," write
that; don't dodge it.

## 7. Pre-flight checklist

Run this before delivering drafted copy. Fix anything that can't be honestly
checked.

- [ ] No fabricated testimonial, logo, statistic, or student count anywhere
      — real, placeholdered, or omitted
- [ ] Every generic-hype phrase caught and replaced with something concrete
      (see the banned list in reference/voice-rules.md)
- [ ] Every curriculum module states a real learning outcome, not just a
      topic name
- [ ] At least one section admits a real limitation, trade-off, or "this
      isn't for everyone" moment
- [ ] The instructor bio has a specific, personal detail, not only a
      credentials list
- [ ] Emphasis (bold/italic) sits on words or phrases, not full sentences,
      and isn't overused
- [ ] Emoji count is proportionate — genuine beats only, not decoration
- [ ] Read it aloud: does it sound like a specific person talking to one
      reader, or a brochure talking to a market segment?
- [ ] The FAQ answers real objections plainly, including at least one "no"
      or named limitation
- [ ] Nothing here would embarrass the instructor if a student fact-checked
      it
